## 1. Overview

Two scholarly traditions converge on the project's core question — *why do religious institutions accumulate and deploy resources, and what determines who gets what?* — but they have barely spoken to each other.

The first is the **religious-economy / rational-choice school** in the sociology of religion (Stark, Bainbridge, Finke, Iannaccone), which treats religious organizations as firms competing in a marketplace, and explains their resource strategies through supply-side competition, club-goods logic, and the economics of commitment. It is almost entirely modern in its empirical base (American congregations, contemporary religious markets).

The second is **ancient economic history and Assyriology**, which has documented for a century that Mesopotamian and Egyptian temples were among the largest economic actors in their societies — landowners, employers, redistributors, lenders — but has treated this mainly as institutional description, not as a problem for the sociology of religion. The quantitative reconstruction of *divine resource allocation* (which gods got how much, from whom, over time) from administrative records is, to my knowledge, an open gap. The single closest prior work is Michael Kozuh's *The Sacrificial Economy* (2014), which reconstructs the sheep economy of one Neo-Babylonian temple (Eanna at Uruk, ca. 625–520 BCE). Nothing comparable exists for the Ur III period despite a far larger and more granular administrative corpus.

The argument of this review: the Ur III ledger project can make an original contribution to *both* literatures — supplying the religious-economy school with its first large-scale premodern test case, and supplying Assyriology with a sociologically framed account of temple resource flows.

## 2. Religious Economy / Rational Choice School

### 2.1 Origins and core claims

The religious-economy approach applies rational-choice theory to religion, modeling the development and success of organized religions through supply and demand **[verified]**. Its major proponents are William Sims Bainbridge, Roger Finke, Laurence Iannaccone, and Rodney Stark **[verified]**.

The foundational statement is Stark and Bainbridge, *A Theory of Religion* (1987) **[standard]**, which proposed that religions are systems of "compensators" — promises of rewards (including supernatural ones) that organizations supply to religious consumers. The paradigm consolidated with:

- **Finke and Stark (1988)** — the article introducing the "religious economies model," arguing that competition among religious suppliers, not secularization, drives religious vitality **[verified]**.
- **Warner (1993)** — R. Stephen Warner's *American Journal of Sociology* article naming the market perspective the **"new paradigm"** in the sociology of religion **[verified]**.
- **Stark and Finke (2000), *Acts of Faith: Explaining the Human Side of Religion*** — the comprehensive theoretical treatment **[verified]**.
- **Finke and Stark (2005), *The Churching of America, 1776–2005*** — the historical-empirical application **[verified]**.

The core assumption: individuals assign value to religious belief and maximize the benefits they receive when choosing a religious home; religious entrepreneurs (typically clergy) therefore offer an array of religious models to meet diverse needs **[verified]**. Competition between houses of worship makes religious organizations stronger and increases participation **[verified]**. Free markets with dense pluralism produce greater public religious participation than regulated/monopoly markets **[verified]** — the supply-side explanation for high American religiosity versus low Western European participation (regulation deters entry; open economies reward religious entrepreneurs) **[verified]**.

### 2.2 The puzzle of strict churches and Iannaccone's club-goods model

The school's most celebrated theoretical contribution is its explanation of why **strict churches succeed** — a puzzle other models struggled with (Kelley 1972, *Why Conservative Churches Are Growing*, posed it first) **[verified]**.

Laurence Iannaccone's **"Sacrifice and Stigma: Reducing Free-riding in Cults, Communes, and Other Collectives"** (*Journal of Political Economy*, 1992) **[verified]** treats religious goods as **club goods**: excludable but non-rival within the group, hence vulnerable to free-riding (Olson 1965 logic) **[verified]**. Strictness — costly behavioral requirements, dietary and dress restrictions, stigmas on outside activity — solves the collective-action problem two ways:

1. **Substitution effect:** requirements act as a per-unit tax on outside activity, inducing members to substitute time and money toward group activities.
2. **Screening effect:** only the most committed will pay the entry costs, so strict groups select for members least likely to free-ride **[verified]**.

Iannaccone (1994) showed empirically that churches with greater sacrificing and stigmatizing requirements do better on attendance, tithing, and voluntary service **[verified]**. The framework has since been widely applied (Berman 2000, 2003; McBride 2008; Cosgel and Minkler 2004, among others) **[verified]**, and Iannaccone's *Journal of Economic Literature* survey (1998, "Introduction to the Economics of Religion") consolidated the subfield within economics **[standard]**.

**Relevance to Ur III:** The club-goods model predicts that costly participation sustains high-commitment religious institutions. Ur III temple offerings — livestock delivered from across the state to specific gods — are a premodern instance of costly religious contribution at scale. The model gives the project a theoretical vocabulary for asking *why* the offering system worked as a commitment device and what sustained the flows.

### 2.3 Critics and limits

The school has serious critics, and the review must acknowledge them:

- **Chaves and Gorski (2001)**, *Annual Review of Sociology* — the landmark critical review; found the evidence for canonical religious-economies claims "spotty" **[verified]**.
- **Voas, Crockett, and Olson (2002)** — methodological critique of the pluralism-participation link **[verified]**.
- **Bruce (2002)** — the comprehensive pro-secularization rebuttal; read alongside Stark and Finke (2000) the two define the debate **[verified]**.
- **Breault (1989); Olson (1998); Norris and Inglehart (2004); Hill and Olson (2009)** — further empirical challenges **[verified]**.

The school's empirical base is overwhelmingly **modern and American**. This is precisely the project's opening: the religious-economy framework has never been tested against a large-scale premodern administrative dataset.

### 2.4 Congregation size and the Dunbar connection

Directly relevant to the 300-person theory: **"Dunbar's Number goes to Church: The Social Brain Hypothesis as a third strand in the study of church growth"** (journal article, DOI: 10.1177/0084672420906215) **[verified]** derives five predictions from the Social Brain Hypothesis:

1. Congregation size is negatively associated with active member involvement;
2. Congregations led by a single individual are limited to weekly attendance of **~150**;
3. Groups of 150 stratify into sub-groups of ~5, 15, and 50;
4. Growth beyond 150 requires structural reorganization with associated tensions **[verified]**.

Supporting data: UK church census figures show ~85–89% of churches reporting average Sunday attendance under 200, with over 50% under 50 (Brierley 2000, 2014) **[verified]**; Jackson (2002) independently estimated 100–150 adults as the maximum for a single clergyperson **[verified]**.

**Implication:** the published literature independently converges on ~150 as the natural congregation size — the "attendance" half of the 300-person theory. The "300 build it" half (the catchment population that produces the institution) remains the project's original contribution to articulate and test.

## 3. Temples as Economic Institutions (Ancient World)

### 3.1 The temple-state debate (Mesopotamia)

The idea that temples dominated the early Mesopotamian economy originates with **Deimel (1931)** and **Falkenstein (1974)**, who argued that in mid-third-millennium Sumer most or all agricultural land was temple-owned **[verified]**. This "temple-state" (Tempelstaat) hypothesis was built upon by **Polanyi** (in *Trade and Markets in the Early Empires*, 1957, and later work) and **Finley** (*The Ancient Economy*, 1973) **[verified]**.

It has since been **demolished by reconsideration of the evidence**: Gelb, Steinkeller, and Whiting (1991) showed that the earliest land-sale records demonstrate individual (nuclear-family) ownership of real property, with no evidence of a transition from communal to individual ownership — "preclud[ing] any possibility of a mainly familial/communal ownership of land" in the third millennium **[verified]**. The hypothesis is likewise defective for Egypt and Mycenaean Greece **[verified]**.

**What survives the demolition:** temples were *major* economic actors without being *exclusive* ones. The current consensus (Steinkeller 2002; Garfinkle 2004) describes the Ur III economy as a **mixed system**: royal domains existing in parallel with traditional temple estates (placed under governors, *ensi*, usually local elites), alongside informal sectors — private family production, mercantile activity, lending, and kinship-group land (Diakonoff 1974) **[verified]**. Private households as well as temples and palaces engaged in money-lending for economic gain (Garfinkle 2004) **[verified]**.

This matters for the project: the divine-account analysis must not assume temples were the whole economy. Temples were cost centers and redistribution nodes *within* a mixed institutional ecology.

### 3.2 The Ur III temple/household economy

Key specialist literature on the institutional economics of Ur III temples:

- **Maekawa, Kazuya (1996), "The Governor's Family and the 'Temple Households' in Ur III Girsu"** — in *Houses and Households in Ancient Mesopotamia*, proceedings of the 40th Rencontre Assyriologique Internationale (Leiden: Nederlands Historisch-Archaeologisch Instituut) **[verified]**. Maekawa's broader work (from the 1970s onward) established the temple household (*é*) as the fundamental unit of Ur III economic organization. His earlier "The Development of É-MÍ in Lagash during Early Dynastic III" (*Mesopotamia* 8, 1973) traces the institutional roots **[verified]**.
- **Garfinkle, Steven** — on the patrimonial (not rational-bureaucratic) character of the Ur III state: "Was the Ur III State Bureaucratic? Patrimonialism and Bureaucracy in the Ur III Period" and "The Third Dynasty of Ur and the Limits of State Power in Early Mesopotamia" (in Garfinkle and Molina, eds., *From the 21st Century B.C. to the 21st Century A.D.*, Eisenbrauns, 2013) **[verified]**. Garfinkle argues the state set terms and conditions of work but relied on existing local networks of power — directly relevant to whether temple resource flows reflect central planning or local patronage **[verified]**.
- **Steinkeller, Piotr (2004)** — on the two-tiered administrative chain: first-order tasks (sealing/unsealing storage, sorting inventories) versus second-order bookkeeping **[verified]**.
- **Michalowski, Piotr (2013), "Networks of Authority and Power in Ur III Times"** (same Madrid volume) — on the personal-network structure underlying the formal bureaucracy **[verified]**.

### 3.3 The bala system and the fiscal role of temples

The **bala** ("exchange"/rotation) system is the Ur III dynasty's mechanism for collecting livestock, grain, labor, and craft products from its provinces; provinces contributed according to what they produced (Girsu: grain; Umma: leather, reed, wood), and the center funded building projects and paid labor (guruš/geme) from the proceeds **[verified]**.

- **Sharlach, Tonia M. (2004), *Provincial Taxation and the Ur III State*** (Brill, Cuneiform Monographs) **[verified]** — the first major synthesis of the bala, and the first to take an **interarchival approach** (tablets from Umma, Lagash, and Puzriš-Dagan) **[verified]**. Sharlach reconstructs the bala roster, identifies three tiers of provinces (multi-month payers, single-month payers, limited obligations), and — importantly — questions whether obligation disparities reflect size/wealth, noting the core provinces "maintained their own regional identities" rather than forming a monolithic entity **[verified]**. She cites Sallaberger's suggestion that Girsu's anomalous obligations reflect its former political importance **[verified]**.
- The Drehem livestock archive sits at the center of this system: Drehem/Puzriš-Dagan was the clearinghouse through which provincial livestock contributions flowed to their destinations — including temples, the palace, and cultic consumption.

### 3.4 Egyptian temples (comparative)

Egypt provides the best-documented comparative case of temple economic power:

- By the end of the New Kingdom, temples controlled **about one-third of Egypt's productive land and labor force** **[verified]**; the Temple of Amun at Karnak was among the wealthiest institutions in the kingdom **[verified]**.
- Temples managed agricultural estates, livestock, and labor; served as administrative centers for collection, storage, and redistribution of grain, oil, and textiles; and functioned as repositories of wealth (gold, precious stones, trade goods) **[verified]**.
- The **Wilbour Papyrus** (New Kingdom) documents temple land management in detail — plot allocation and tax collection in agricultural produce **[verified]**.
- In Ptolemaic Egypt, an estimated 30–50% of farmland was crown-owned, with temple property another large category; temples managed land, archived information, and owned estates supporting temple expenses **[verified]**.
- The Amen priesthood's economic power at times rivaled the monarchy, with political consequences **[verified]** — the classic case of a religious institution's resource base translating into political leverage.

**Comparative lesson:** Egyptian temples show what happens when a religious institution's economic weight crosses the threshold into political rivalry. The Ur III material lets us ask whether Sumerian temples ever approached that threshold — and the offering data may show the palace *capturing* the temple economy rather than competing with it (the deified king receiving offerings alongside the gods is suggestive).

### 3.5 Sacrifice as economic exchange with gods

The UrbRel research program ("Reading Religion Economically") offers a useful theoretical frame: sacrifice can be construed as an **economic relation in which humans and gods exchange goods and services** — an ox transmitted to the altar in the hope of reciprocation (crop yield, fertility, success) **[verified]**. In ancient Babylonia, the gods themselves were understood to have material needs: in the *Atrahasis* Epic (ca. 1700 BCE), the hungry gods "gathered like flies over the offering" until they had eaten it **[verified]**.

The frame explicitly does *not* require a calculating homo economicus: gifts to gods operate within gift-exchange logic — the social relation is safeguarded, favor cannot be "bought" **[verified]**. This is compatible with the religious-economy school's supply-side language while avoiding anachronistic market reductionism.

Charles Eisenstein's *Sacred Economics* (2011) makes the popular version of the temple-as-redistribution-hub argument (gift networks → centralized redistribution via temple, then palace) **[verified]** — useful for TED-framing, not for scholarly citation.

## 4. Quantitative and Archaeological Approaches

### 4.1 The closest prior art: Kozuh's *Sacrificial Economy*

**Kozuh, Michael (2014), *The Sacrificial Economy: Assessors, Contractors, and Thieves in the Management of Sacrificial Sheep at the Eanna Temple of Uruk (ca. 625–520 B.C.)*** (Eisenbrauns, Explorations in Ancient Near Eastern Civilizations) **[verified]** is the single most relevant predecessor:

- The Eanna temple sacrificed a **minimum of nine lambs daily** in routine offerings, up to **90 in a single day** on festivals — about **4,300 lambs per year** **[verified]**.
- **120+ herdsmen** were connected to the Eanna; tens of thousands of sheep and goats under temple responsibility **[verified]**.
- Kozuh reconstructs the full economic organization: herdsmen delivered male lambs for sacrifice and wool (sold in bulk), through a system of assessors and contractors, analyzed from bookkeeping records, management records, and legal documents **[verified]**.

**Why it matters:** Kozuh proves the method works — administrative records *can* yield a quantified account of a temple's sacrificial economy. **Why the Ur III project is still original:** Kozuh covers one temple, one commodity (sheep), one century (Neo-Babylonian). The Ur III project covers the *state-level* system, multiple commodities, and — critically — compares allocation *across gods and temples*, which Kozuh does not attempt.

### 4.2 Cultic calendars and the quantification of ritual

**Sallaberger, Walther (1993), *Der kultische Kalender der Ur III-Zeit*** (Berlin: de Gruyter) **[verified]** is the authoritative reconstruction of the Ur III cultic calendar — month by month, city by city (Lagaš/Girsu, Nippur, Ur, Umma), listing the cultic events known for each month **[verified]**. A 2021 follow-up extends the calendar work *(Studia Chaburensia 9)* **[verified]**.

Sallaberger's work establishes *what the ritual obligations were*. What it does not do — and what the ledger project can — is measure *what was actually delivered against those obligations*, in animals and grain, per deity, per year. The gap between prescribed cult and recorded disbursement is exactly where a quantitative ledger adds value.

Related: the review of *The Cultic Calendars of the Ancient Near East* notes the field's dependence on Sallaberger for all Ur III-period sources **[verified]**.

### 4.3 Accounting history and the "first accountants" literature

A smaller literature in accounting history treats Mesopotamian temple administration as the origin of the discipline:

- **Mattessich (1998)** and the accounting-history surveys (e.g., Carmona and Ezzamel) argue that Ur III accounting — with theoretical vs. actual performance, debit posts, standardized obligations, and budgetary procedures — represents the most developed form of third-millennium accountability **[verified]**.
- **Nissen, Damerow, and Englund (1993)** on archaic bookkeeping: the checking of actual against theoretical amounts was "perhaps the most important accounting operation introduced during the third millennium BC" **[verified]**.
- **Schmandt-Besserat (1992)** on tokens and the origins of writing; **Englund (2004)** on proto-cuneiform account-books **[verified]**.
- **Ingham (2004), *The Nature of Money***: Mesopotamian temples and palaces used the shekel as a money of account to organize flows of goods and labor services, with rents and taxes recorded in money of account but paid in goods and labor **[verified]**.

This literature supports the Accounting Historians Journal paper: the Ur III material is not just data *for* sociology of religion, it is evidence in the history of accounting itself.

### 4.4 Archaeological quantification

Quantitative archaeology of ritual economy exists but operates at a different resolution: faunal assemblages from temple precincts (fish-bone layers in early temples, e.g.) **[standard]**, the Lagash Archaeological Project's regional economic integration work **[verified]**, and settlement-pattern studies of temple-centered urbanization **[standard]**. These measure *deposits*; the tablet archives measure *transactions*. The two have rarely been joined — another opportunity.

## 5. Gaps and Opportunities

### 5.1 Has anyone measured divine resource allocation from administrative records?

**To my knowledge, no — not for the Ur III period, and not comparatively across deities.** The components exist in isolation:

| Component | Status |
|---|---|
| Cultic calendar (what was prescribed) | Sallaberger 1993 — done, authoritative |
| Single-temple sacrificial economy (sheep) | Kozuh 2014 — done, but Neo-Babylonian, one temple, one commodity |
| Provincial taxation flows | Sharlach 2004 — done, but fiscal, not divine-allocation |
| Temple households as economic units | Maekawa 1996 and predecessors — done, institutional |
| State-level ledger with conservation checks | **Open** — this project |
| Ranked divine recipients with quantities over time | **Open** — this project |
| Test of religious-economy theory on premodern data | **Open** — this project |

The specific unanswered empirical questions the ledger can address:

1. **Ranking:** Which gods received the most livestock/grain, and is the ranking stable across the ~40-year Drehem sequence or does it shift with reigns and politics?
2. **Theology vs. geography:** Do local city gods (Baba at Girsu, Šara at Umma, Nanna at Ur) dominate their home archives, or does a state-level pantheon hierarchy override local preference?
3. **The royal cult:** What share went to the deified king (Šulgi) versus traditional gods — a quantitative measure of ruler cult as fiscal instrument?
4. **Prescription vs. practice:** Do disbursements track Sallaberger's cultic calendar, or do shortfalls and substitutions reveal fiscal stress (especially in the Ibbi-Suen collapse years)?
5. **Redistribution:** How much of what entered temples flowed back out (rations, wages, resale of wool) — i.e., were temples accumulation points or throughput nodes?

### 5.2 Theoretical opportunities

- **A premodern test of the religious-economy school.** The school's predictions about competition, strictness, and commitment have never been confronted with a full administrative record of a state religious economy. Even a negative result (the framework doesn't fit a non-market, non-voluntaristic system) would be informative — it would delimit the theory's scope conditions.
- **Club goods without voluntarism.** Iannaccone's model assumes voluntary membership. Ur III temple contributions were tax-like (bala) *and* votive. The project can explore how commitment logic operates when participation is compelled — do compelled contributions still function as costly signals?
- **The 300-person theory.** The Dunbar-church literature independently supports ~150 as the natural congregation size. The project's contribution is the *formation* threshold (300 people → house of worship) and the *economic* mechanism (3 wealthy households sustain a professional minister). The GRID dataset is the test bed; the Ur III material is the deep-historical illustration that religious institutions have always been economic institutions first.

### 5.3 What this review could not verify

- Whether Sallaberger or others have produced **ranked quantitative totals** of offerings per deity for Ur III (my reading is no, but the German monograph should be checked directly before asserting this).
- The full contents of Kozuh's argument beyond the publisher's description (the book itself should be consulted).
- Whether any **unpublished dissertations** have attempted divine-allocation quantification from CDLI data (a ProQuest/WorldCat search is warranted).
- Maekawa's Japanese-language publications, which may contain quantitative temple-household data not reflected in the English summaries.
